Contingency tables

Contingency tables and crosstabs with the tests that belong to them

Two categorical columns, and a table of counts. The interesting part is not building the table but choosing the right test for it — chi-square when expected counts are healthy, an exact test when they are not — and then finding out which cells drive the association.

The honest part

The right test depends on the counts, not the variable types

A contingency table crosses two categorical variables and counts the observations in each cell. Pearson's chi-square tests whether the row and column variables are independent. When the expected counts are small, that test's p-value is unreliable, so the app offers the alternatives: Fisher's exact test for 2×2 tables, the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test (Monte Carlo) for larger tables, and the likelihood-ratio G-test. It warns you when expected counts fall below 5 rather than quietly reporting a chi-square anyway.

A significant test says there is an association but not where. For that, Magic Stat reports post-hoc work by cell: adjusted standardized residuals (with a multiple-comparison correction), each cell's chi-square contribution, and Freeman–Tukey deviates. A table with a significant chi-square and no cell analysis is half an answer.

Effect size matters as much as the p-value, because with a large N a trivial association can be significant. Cramér's V, the phi coefficient (2×2) and the contingency coefficient are reported alongside, so the size of the association is visible and not left to the p-value to imply.

What it does

What Magic Stat gives you for contingency tables

  • The full table: observed frequencies with expected counts under independence, plus row, column and total percentages.
  • Association tests: Pearson's chi-square (with optional Yates' correction for 2×2), the likelihood-ratio G-test, Fisher's exact test for 2×2, the Fisher–Freeman–Halton Monte Carlo exact test for larger tables (with a chi-square approximation fallback), and the Mantel–Haenszel linear-by-linear test.
  • Effect sizes: Cramér's V, phi (2×2) and the contingency coefficient, each with an interpretation.
  • Post-hoc by cell: adjusted standardized residuals, per-cell chi-square contributions and Freeman–Tukey deviates, on their own tabs.
  • Choice of correction for the post-hoc: Bonferroni, Holm–Bonferroni, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR (default), Benjamini–Yekutieli FDR, or none.
  • An assumption warning when the minimum expected count is below 5, so the chi-square is not used blindly.
How it works

From two categorical columns to the cells that matter

  1. Load the data and pick the row and column variables. both categorical, with at least two categories each.
  2. Set the options. Yates' correction for 2×2, the post-hoc correction, and whether to show percentages and expected counts.
  3. Run. the table and every applicable test are computed in one step.
  4. Check the assumptions. if the expected counts are small, read the exact test instead of the chi-square.
  5. Read the cells. use the residuals and contributions to say which categories drive the association, then export the tables and the report.
Options

The settings, in the dialog

Tests run: Pearson chi-square · likelihood ratio (G) · Fisher exact (2×2) · Fisher–Freeman–Halton Monte Carlo (r×c) · Mantel–Haenszel linear-by-linear.

Yates' correction: on or off, for 2×2 tables only (on by default).

Post-hoc correction: Bonferroni · Holm–Bonferroni · Benjamini–Hochberg FDR · Benjamini–Yekutieli FDR · None.

Display: row %, column % and expected counts, each toggleable.

Frequently asked

Contingency table questions, answered honestly

Chi-square or Fisher's exact?

Use chi-square when the expected counts are all reasonably large (roughly ≥ 5). When they are not, its p-value is approximate and Fisher's exact — or, for tables larger than 2×2, the Fisher–Freeman–Halton Monte Carlo exact test — is the right choice. The app flags the assumption so you can make that call.

What does the likelihood-ratio G-test add?

It tests the same independence hypothesis with a different statistic. The two usually agree; they can diverge with small counts, and having both lets you see whether the conclusion depends on the specific test.

How do I know which cells are responsible?

Look at the adjusted standardized residuals and the cell contributions on the post-hoc tabs. A cell with a large adjusted residual departs from independence more than chance would explain, after the correction you selected.

Why can a small association be significant?

Because the chi-square grows with sample size. A significant test with a small Cramér's V means the association is real but weak — report the effect size, not only the p-value.

Is the Fisher Monte Carlo result stable?

The r×c exact test is estimated by Monte Carlo simulation with fixed margins, so the p-value is an estimate rather than an exact enumeration. It is the standard approach for tables where exact enumeration is infeasible, and it is reported as a Monte Carlo result.
