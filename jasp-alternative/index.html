The honest part

What JASP does better than Magic Stat

JASP is free and open source, and its Bayesian analyses are its signature. Bayes factors for t-tests, ANOVA, regression and contingency tables, posterior estimation and prior handling — all available from menus, with documentation and a textbook to match. If Bayesian statistics behind a point-and-click interface is what you need, JASP covers it more completely than Magic Stat does.

Magic Stat also has Bayesian tools — a JZS Bayes factor for the t-test, a Bayes factor for correlation, an approximate factor for one-way ANOVA, and posterior summaries with HDI and ROPE — but they are computed by direct sampling of conjugate models, not by MCMC. There is no Stan or PyMC underneath. That is a real difference, and for serious Bayesian modelling JASP (or R) is the more capable choice.

Where Magic Stat aims to be useful is the rest of the paper: a broad frequentist toolkit across ecology, econometrics, psychometrics and the medical sciences, all in one application, with the APA table and the report coming out of the same run.

What it does

What Magic Stat adds around the Bayesian analysis

  • Frequentist breadth in one place: the ANOVA family, the regression family, GLM/GAM, mixed models and GEE, SEM and PLS-SEM, psychometrics, meta-analysis, time series, ecology (PERMANOVA, ANOSIM, Mantel), econometrics, medical statistics and spatial analysis.
  • Descriptive and inferential output formatted for a paper — mean (SD), exact p-values and compact letter displays, copied as text into Word or LibreOffice.
  • Publication figures exported as PNG, PDF or SVG, with legends that can sit outside the plot.
  • A report per analysis, with the method, the settings and the interpretation, exportable to .docx, .html or .md.
  • No code. Point-and-click throughout, in the same style that made JASP popular.
  • Local by default. The analysis runs on your computer; nothing is sent anywhere.
How it works

Choose, run, read, export

  1. Open a spreadsheet. CSV or Excel; the data is not converted or reformatted.
  2. Choose the analysis. frequentist or Bayesian, from the Statistics menu.
  3. Set the options. every setting you would otherwise pass as an argument is a control in the dialog.
  4. Read the result. table, figure and a plain-language interpretation, together.
  5. Export. table to Word, figure to the gallery, report to .docx/.html/.md.
Options

The Bayesian tools, stated precisely

Bayesian in Magic Stat: JZS Bayes factor for the t-test (one sample, paired, two groups); a Bayes factor for correlation; an approximate Bayes factor (BIC) for one-way ANOVA with pairwise Bayesian comparisons; and posterior estimates with HDI, ROPE and direction probability, from direct sampling of conjugate models.

Honest limit: no MCMC sampler — no hierarchical Bayesian models, no custom priors beyond the built-in ones. For those, JASP and R (with Stan, brms or rstanarm) are the right tools.

Everything else in the app is frequentist, and that is the bulk of it — from t-tests to SEM.

Frequently asked

JASP questions, answered honestly

Is JASP free?

Yes — free and open source. If it fits your work, use it; this page is not a reason to leave it.

Does Magic Stat have Bayesian statistics?

Yes, but a narrower set: Bayes factors and posterior summaries without an MCMC sampler. JASP's Bayesian module is deeper and better documented, and for hierarchical models it is the better choice.

Then why choose Magic Stat?

For the frequentist breadth and the manuscript workflow: methods from ecology, econometrics, psychometrics and medicine in one application, with APA tables and an automatic report.

Is it open source?

No. Magic Stat is a commercial application — a free 48-hour trial with no card, then US$1,400 per year.

Do my data leave my computer?

No. The analysis runs locally; the only automatic signal is an anonymous installation counter with no data in it.
